## The setup

My college runs a Design Thinking Module across semesters five, six and seven. The
department assigns you a team of students from different branches and three mentor
professors, also from different departments, and gives you about ten months to define,
design and build something.

Our team was five people across three disciplines — two from EEE, two from CSE, and me from
ECE. None of us had met before.

The structure is strict. **DTM-1** is problem definition only: survey, research, and proof
that the problem is worth solving, approved first by our mentors and then by external
examiners. **DTM-2** is the plan — a step-by-step implementation guide, presented weekly.
**DTM-3** is the build, and it ends in a showcase to a judging panel.

Nobody volunteered to lead, so I did.

## The first problem was us

Before we could pick a project, everyone wanted to build the thing their own department was
good at. The CSE guys wanted a web project they could code. The EEE guys wanted to build a
new kind of transformer. Everyone had a favourite and no one was listening.

So I asked each person to come back with three points about their own idea:

1. Is it worth solving?
2. How does the team contribute to it — all of us, not just you?
3. Why should the others' ideas *not* be picked?

The third question is the one that changed the room. Once you have to say out loud why
someone else's idea is weaker, you have to actually engage with it, and you stop defending
your own out of habit.

After that discussion we stopped thinking as four departments and started thinking as a
team. The roles fell out on their own: CSE on the software, me and the EEE guys on the
hardware prototype, and all of us on the part where the code has to talk to the hardware.

## Finding the problem

We went through a lot of ideas and rejected most of them in brainstorming. What we kept
coming back to was road accidents — the numbers are enormous and nothing about it is a
solved problem.

But "accidents are bad" isn't a project. DTM-1 exists to force you to prove there is a
specific problem worth building for, so we ran a survey. A Google Form circulated online,
and offline we walked around campus asking people to scan the QR and fill it in.

<Metric value="500+" caveat="survey responses, collected online and in person on campus" />

Two things came out of it.

**One question mattered more than the rest.** We asked whether the respondent, or someone
they knew, had been involved in an accident. Most people said yes. That single answer took
the project from a statistic we'd read somewhere to something that had touched most of the
people around us, and it made every decision after it easier to justify.

**And one person stopped filling in the form to talk to me.** He told me about a friend who
crashed on a highway on a rainy night. Nobody saw it happen. He was unconscious and lay
there for a long time before a passing truck driver noticed him and got him to a hospital,
by which point he had lost a lot of blood.

That story is the reason the device doesn't wait for anyone to press anything.

## What the research told us to build

The survey and market research pointed at three causes behind accident deaths:

1. **Delay in alerting emergency services**
2. **Alcohol consumption**
3. **Late-night drowsiness**

All three were identified in DTM-1, before we built anything. We then designed one device
that addressed all three:

- **On crash detection**, it immediately sends a message with the accident location to
  pre-set family and friend numbers. It doesn't need the victim to be conscious.
- **If alcohol is detected** on the driver, it locks the engine.
- **If drowsiness is detected**, it wakes the driver with a loud alert.

## Two arguments I had to win

**The mentors didn't agree with each other.** Some thought the problem was worth solving and
believed we could do it. At least one wasn't convinced it was needed, or that students could
build something that complex. She asked us to come back within a week with a demo. We
presented the concrete steps of how we'd get there, and she approved it.

**The CSE team wanted facial recognition for drowsiness detection.** It was the better
solution and I agreed with them in principle. But training a model, integrating it with the
rest of the system, and doing it inside a fixed academic deadline — with the cost that came
with it — wasn't realistic.

We talked it through and went with eye-blink sensors instead. Less ambitious, and it worked.

## How the patent happened

There was no separate pitch, which surprised me.

After the DTM-3 showcase, the judging panel decides which projects are worth filing a patent
on and recommends them to the college IPR Cell. The IPR Cell then asks for a written
document explaining the innovation. We wrote it and sent it in.

They accepted it as novel because of **three problem solutions embedded in a single
device** — not any one of the three on its own.

Most of the other teams didn't get through, and I think I know why. The safe move is to take
an existing project, change a few things, finish it and collect the marks. We and our mentors
were aiming at something new from the beginning. Novelty is the whole test for a patent, so
the teams that optimised for marks were never going to clear it.

**Indian Patent Application No. 202441049990 — Published.**
Also published as a conference paper at ICADIE-2024.

## What I'd do differently

**The ML model.** I still think facial-recognition drowsiness detection would have made the
invention meaningfully stronger. I made the right call at the time — I couldn't risk the
deadline and our marks on it — but it's the one thing I'd go back for if the constraint
weren't there.

**Documentation, and this one actually changed how I work.** We put everything into demos,
slide decks and the prototype. We wrote almost nothing down as we went. Then at the end of
DTM-3 we had to submit a full detailed report covering all three modules from start to
finish, and we had to reconstruct ten months of work in one sitting. It was miserable, and
it was entirely our own doing.

I document as I go now. Two years later, as an SDE, I still do it — I write things down
while they're fresh so future-me doesn't have to rebuild them from memory. That's the
lesson from this project I actually use.
