## The library was the problem, not the product

I work on the iOS and tvOS apps for VPlayed, an OTT streaming platform. One of the
catalogues I worked on is mostly indie films — small filmmakers, no marketing budget, not
titles anyone arrives already looking for.

It's a genuinely good library. That was the frustrating part. As an admin I could filter by
genre, sort by what was being watched, and find things worth watching in a few minutes.

Users couldn't do any of that. They got a home screen and a search bar.

I had a discovery tool the audience didn't have. Once I noticed that, the problem stopped
being "how do we get people to watch more" and became "how do people find the good films
when there's nothing telling them which ones those are."

## What the data was already showing

Two things pointed the same way.

In the admin analytics, each title has its own numbers. Titles with higher share counts
also had higher watch counts. Sharing and watching moved together.

And people were already sharing — the app had ratings and comments on each title, and some
users were copying links and sending individual films to friends. One title at a time.

That last detail is what started it. Sharing one film at a time is slow. My first thought
wasn't a social feature at all. It was just: let people share more than one film at once.

## Why not build recommendations

The obvious answer to "users can't find the good content" is a recommendation engine.
Learn what someone likes, suggest more of it.

I argued against it, for two reasons.

The first is about the content. These are indie films. If the app tells you something is
"most watched", that doesn't mean much — you've never heard of it, and you know the app
wants you to click. If a friend tells you a film is good, you'll probably watch it. For
long-tail content, word of mouth carries far more weight than anything the platform says
about itself.

A film like Avengers doesn't need this. It's already known, already promoted everywhere.
Small films are exactly where a recommendation from a person beats a recommendation from an
algorithm.

The second reason was cost. A personalised recommendation engine is a large amount of work
before it returns anything useful. Sharing was much less effort and, for this catalogue, I
thought it would deliver more.

## What shipped

It started as bulk sharing and turned into something bigger.

**Watchlists.** Create a list, keep adding titles to it over time.

**Two ways to share, deliberately different.** Share to a *collaborator* and the other
person can add and remove titles — you build the list together. Share *publicly* and anyone
can view it, like it, comment on it, and share it on again.

**Follow other users.** If someone's taste matches yours, you can follow them and keep up
with the lists they make.

**A profile page**, close to Instagram's — how many watchlists you've made, followers,
following.

**A "New Watchlists" tab** on the home screen, where admins feature lists made by users so
everyone else can browse them.

The last one mattered more than I expected. It gave good lists somewhere to be found
instead of relying on them being passed around privately.

## What happened, including the part I can't explain

Views went up. People who opened a watchlist usually didn't watch one film from it — they
watched several. That was the effect I was aiming for and it worked.

The part I didn't predict: **new users started arriving.** The follow mechanics turned into
a reason to bring people in. Users invited friends to join so they'd have someone to share
lists with and someone to follow. I built a retention feature and it started acquiring
users.

I don't know exactly why it worked as well as it did. We never established the cause
properly.

## About the 12%

The watchlist shipped in a release that also included EPG improvements and HLS streaming
optimisations. Retention across that release rose 12%.

**I can't tell you how much of that was the watchlist.** The three changes weren't
instrumented separately, so all I can honestly say is that they shipped together and the
number moved.

I'd rather say that than claim the whole 12%.

## What I'd do differently

Instrument it before shipping, not after.

If I built this again I'd put proper event tracking in from the start — PostHog, AppsFlyer,
Amplitude — and map the actual user journey. Then I could answer the questions I still
can't: how many people made a list, how many followed someone, how many new users came from
an invite, and what share of the 12% was mine.

The feature worked. I just can't prove which part of it did.
